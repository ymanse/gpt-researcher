# Solid-State Battery Supply Chain: Manufacturing Bottlenecks and Key Players

## Executive Summary

Solid-state batteries (SSBs) replace the flammable liquid electrolyte and polymer separator of conventional lithium-ion cells with a solid lithium-ion conductor, promising higher energy density, faster charging, and improved safety. The supply chain that must be built to deliver them, however, is fundamentally different from today's lithium-ion chain: it requires new upstream materials (lithium sulfide, sulfide/oxide/polymer solid electrolytes), new separator-film manufacturing processes, and new cell-assembly and quality-control regimes. IDTechEx concludes that "New material/component selection and change of manufacturing procedures indicate a reshuffle of the battery supply chain, such as lithium metal anode, silicon anode, and solid-state electrolyte" [10]. This report maps the three electrolyte chemistry routes and their manufacturing constraints, the separator/film scale-up problem, the emerging materials supply chain, the key players and partnerships, and the still-divergent commercialization timelines.

## 1. Electrolyte Chemistry Routes and Their Manufacturing Constraints

Three solid-electrolyte families dominate industrial development, each with a distinct manufacturing bottleneck profile, as summarized by Fraunhofer ISI: "Polymer electrolytes are inexpensive and easy to process, but have low ionic conductivities at room temperature," while "Sulfide electrolytes offer similarly high ionic conductivities as liquid electrolytes, but generally show limited (electro)chemical stability towards cathode and anode active materials," and "Oxide electrolytes show high (electro)chemical stability towards cathode and anode active materials, but are complex to process and have only low ionic conductivities" [3].

**Polymer route.** Polymer SSBs are the only chemistry with a long commercial track record — Blue Solutions "has been manufacturing polymer SSBs for a long time, offering SSB products for EVs" [10], mainly in electric buses — but the cells "must be operated at elevated temperatures in order to ensure sufficient ionic conductivity" [3], which confines them to niches.

**Sulfide route.** Sulfides are the choice of Toyota, Samsung SDI, and Solid Power because of their liquid-like conductivity and because "Sulfide solid electrolytes are characterized by softness and adhesiveness to other materials, which is suitable for battery mass production" [1]. Their manufacturing constraints are severe, however. QuantumScape — a competitor with an obvious commercial interest, but citing published data — notes that sulfides react with moisture: "when they come into contact with water or humidity, they produce a gas known as hydrogen sulfide," so "Keeping water away from sulfide materials during manufacturing is a serious, complex, and expensive challenge" [5]. Sulfide cells have also historically needed high stack pressure to suppress dendrites: "Samsung tested its sulfide-based separator batteries under 20-40 atmospheres of pressure, Solid Power reported testing at 70-90 atmospheres," and a Harvard lab published results "at more than 750 atmospheres," whereas QuantumScape argues "anything over 10 atmospheres is likely to be impractical in an EV battery pack" [5]. Sulfide developers dispute the severity of these constraints, and KLA gives the more neutral framing: "Sulfide-based solid electrolytes, despite offering high ionic conductivity at room temperature, face significant interface issues and lithium dendrite growth" [4].

**Oxide route.** Oxide/ceramic electrolytes (QuantumScape, ProLogium, ION Storage Systems) avoid the H2S hazard and are chemically robust, but "are difficult to process due to their brittleness and high sintering temperatures" [4]. A Nature Energy processing review puts hard numbers on this: garnet-type LLZO requires a "high sintering temperature (~1,000 °C)," and widespread adoption requires "cost-effective processing and the integration of solid electrolytes of about the same thickness as the polymer-membrane separators found in conventional lithium-ion" cells — with target electrolyte processing costs below roughly US$4 per square meter for cost parity with lithium-ion [13].

## 2. The Separator/Film Scale-Up Bottleneck

Whatever the chemistry, the central manufacturing bottleneck is producing a thin, defect-free solid-electrolyte film at high speed. KLA, which is porting semiconductor process control into battery fabs, states that "The solid electrolyte can be only 20 microns thick, introducing dimensions and standards new to the battery industry," and that "Defectivity is a key barrier to reaching the solid-state battery HVM phase" — physical defects, impurities, pinholes, cracks, or uneven topography can seed dendrites that short the cell [4]. Reliability compounds the problem because SSBs "consist of tens to hundreds of layers built or stacked on top of each other, with no redundancy" [4], so per-layer yield multiplies across the stack.

Film throughput is where the leaders are now competing. QuantumScape's answer is its Cobra ceramic-separator process, which in June 2025 entered baseline cell production: "Compared to the prior-generation Raptor process, Cobra offers a ~25x improvement in heat treatment speed and occupies a fraction of the physical space per film start – key advantages in the design of a scalable gigafactory production line," forming "the foundation of QS's high-throughput, continuous-flow separator production platform" and "laying the groundwork for higher-volume B1 sample production" [6]. On the sulfide side, Solid Power "operates a roll-to-roll production line in Colorado, producing prototype all-solid-state lithium metal batteries" [10], and ProLogium's Taoyuan plant — described by IDTechEx as "the world's first gigafactory for solid-state lithium ceramic batteries" — uses "continuous wet coating for solid electrolytes" and has achieved "a production efficiency 2.6 times greater than ProLogium's earlier facilities" [10].

## 3. Upstream Materials: The Emerging Electrolyte Supply Chain

The clearest supply-chain choke point today is precursor and electrolyte volume. For the sulfide route the critical precursor is lithium sulfide (Li2S), and the anchor investment is Idemitsu Kosan's plant at its Chiba refinery: "The facility will have a production capacity of 1,000 metric tons of lithium sulphide annually — enough to supply solid electrolyte for 50,000–60,000 EVs," to be completed "by June 2027, with an estimated cost of 21.3 billion yen ($143 million)" [2]. Idemitsu executive Tetsuji Mishina named the core economic bottleneck directly: "A major challenge for global adoption of solid-state batteries is lowering solid electrolyte costs" [2]. Idemitsu makes Li2S "using by-products which are generated in the course of petroleum refining" [1], and in January 2026 "announced plans to construct a pilot facility to manufacture sulfide-based solid electrolytes for Toyota's EV solid-state batteries" [11].

Current electrolyte volumes illustrate how early the chain still is. Solid Power — whose business model is "selling its electrolyte to cell manufacturers and licensing its cell designs and manufacturing processes" [7] — reported for 2023 that it "Achieved electrolyte production capability of 1.1 metric tons per month" [7], i.e. roughly 13 tons/year. Its 10-K plans to raise annual capacity "first to 75 metric tons in 2026 and then to 140 metric tons in 2028" [8], and its June 2026 investor update confirms it "targets 30 metric tons of annual pilot electrolyte capacity from two lines" and "expects to reach 75 metric tons of annual capacity by year-end 2026" [9]. Under its January 2024 agreements, "SK On is required to purchase at least eight metric tons of electrolyte from Solid Power through 2030" [8]. Set against Idemitsu's 1,000 t/y of precursor for only ~50,000–60,000 EVs [2], these tonnages show electrolyte supply is orders of magnitude short of gigawatt-hour-scale demand. Cathode materials are also being locked up: Toyota's newsroom lists an October 2025 collaboration in which "Sumitomo Metal Mining and Toyota Collaborate on Mass Production of Cathode Materials for All-Solid-State Batteries" [1].

## 4. Key Players and Partnerships

- **Toyota + Idemitsu (Japan, sulfide):** the deepest vertical partnership; the two firms are "world leaders in patent registration numbers relating to all-solid-state batteries and sulfide solid electrolytes" and have "established a task force consisting of dozens of members" to industrialize electrolyte production [1].
- **QuantumScape (US, oxide ceramic separator, lithium-metal anode):** backed by Volkswagen; in November 2025 it announced "validation of its next-generation 24-layer prototype cell through third-party automotive testing, demonstrating consistent fast-charging capability from 10% to 80% in under 15 minutes" [12].
- **Solid Power (US, sulfide electrolyte merchant supplier):** partners include BMW, Ford and SK On; it "delivered A-1 Sample cells in October" 2023, "formally entering the automotive qualification process" [7] and in October 2025 "expanded its sulfide-based electrolyte production line, increasing output capacity to support multiple automakers conducting B-sample cell testing" [12].
- **Samsung SDI (Korea, sulfide):** operates the "S-Line" pilot at Suwon and "plans to achieve mass production of all-solid-state batteries by 2027" [10]; Korea's SK Innovation, LG Chem and Samsung SDI "are collaborating on next-generation battery research" [10].
- **ProLogium (Taiwan, ceramic):** its Taoyuan gigafactory "began supplying automakers in 2024" [10].
- **ION Storage Systems (US, anode-less oxide):** its Maryland pilot line is "expected to produce 1 MWh of battery cells, scaling up to 10 MWh by early 2025 and aiming for 500 MWh capacity by 2028" [10].
- **Chinese entrants:** BYD, CATL and FAW are pushing semi-solid and hybrid routes; in February 2026 "FAW achieved 500 Wh/kg energy density using a low-cost hybrid lithium-manganese solid-state battery pack" [11].

## 5. Commercialization Timelines: Where Players and Analysts Disagree

Timelines diverge, and the differences matter for supply-chain investment:

- **Toyota (company position):** "market launch of BEVs with all-solid-state batteries in 2027-28" [1]. **Japan's METI (validating body, more conservative on scale):** "validates Toyota's plan to begin solid-state battery production by 2026, with mass production projected around 2030," with early narratives citing "potential 1,000 km range and 10-minute charging claims" [11]. Toyota's own September 2025 in-vehicle testing reported "a projected driving range of more than 1,000 km per charge under controlled test conditions" [12].
- **Samsung SDI:** mass production "by 2027" [10] — the most aggressive large-cell-maker target.
- **BYD:** "targeted solid-state batteries by 2027, framing it as a next step beyond current lithium-ion," with pacing "dependent on scaling, validation, and cost reduction across supply chains" [11].
- **Solid Power:** "plans to scale up its operations and achieve full mass production by 2030" [10] — notably later than the sulfide OEM headlines.
- **Hyundai:** "Partial mass production is expected by 2027, with full-scale operations targeted for 2030" [10].
- **Analysts:** Fraunhofer ISI expects sulfide and oxide cell concepts "on the market between 2025 and 2030," with oxide SSBs initially entering "as hybrid (liquid/solid) cell concepts" and an overall "trend towards hybridization" [3]; Future Markets sees major developers' "mass production timelines converging on 2027–2028" and projects the market to "grow at compound annual growth rates of 33–57% through 2036" [14], while IDTechEx frames obstacles as addressable "within this decade" [10].

## 6. Outlook

The bottleneck hierarchy is consistent across sources: (1) solid-electrolyte cost and volume — Idemitsu's 1,000 t/y plant and Solid Power's 75→140 t/y roadmap are pilot-scale against EV demand [2][8]; (2) thin-film separator manufacturing at yield — ~20 µm defect-free films across hundreds of stacked layers with no redundancy [4], requiring step-change processes like Cobra's ~25x faster heat treatment [6]; (3) chemistry-specific constraints — H2S handling and stack pressure for sulfides [5], ~1,000 °C sintering and brittleness for oxides [4][13]. Whoever solves electrolyte cost ("lowering solid electrolyte costs" [2]) and separator yield first will anchor the reshuffled supply chain; today the sulfide chain (Toyota–Idemitsu–Sumitomo, Samsung SDI, Solid Power–SK On) and the ceramic chain (QuantumScape–VW, ProLogium, ION Storage) are being built in parallel, with credible first commercial vehicles in 2027–28 but analyst and government expectations placing true mass production closer to 2030 [1][10][11].
